## DLite: History-Free Speculative Decoding That Deletes the Drafter's KV Cache

> **TL;DR** — DLite is a speculative decoding framework in which the draft model stores no history and instead reuses the target model's hidden states directly to perform purely local prediction. On agentic workloads it raises acceptance length by up to about 70% over DSpark while **completely eliminating** the cost of maintaining a draft KV cache.

## Core Idea

Speculative decoding is a technique in which a lightweight draft model first produces candidate tokens and the target model verifies them in parallel in a single forward pass, accepting several tokens at once (source: §2.1). DLite's central observation can be summed up in one sentence.

> Existing drafters must independently re-encode and maintain the **same context the target model has already processed**, and this both (1) collapses acceptance length and (2) incurs the cost of maintaining a draft KV cache (source: §1, §2.2).

DLite inverts this observation and designs the drafter to consume the target model's hidden states directly, **without reconstructing any history at all**. It turns "the problem of modeling an ever-growing history" into "a fixed-size local prediction problem independent of length" (source: §3). Three components realize this.

- **History-free parallel backbone** — takes the target hidden states + anchor token + K placeholders and generates representations for K positions in parallel, storing no draft KV cache (source: §3.1).
- **Lightweight sequential head** — restores only the intra-block causal dependencies that parallel generation loses, using a low-rank RNN (source: §3.1).
- **Two-stage training strategy** — shows that direct KL distillation between structurally different drafter and target fails, and introduces a warm-up/stabilization two-stage scheme that uses an isomorphic teacher as a bridge (source: §3.2, Fig. 4).

## Background: The Problem They Solved

LLMs have become the substrate for agents in coding, web search, healthcare, and more, and agent execution leads to frequent LLM calls. Speculative decoding is already a mainstream acceleration technique adopted in DeepSeek-V4, SGLang, vLLM, and others (source: §1). But the authors point out that agentic workloads differ fundamentally from conventional conversational inference: **long input, short output, frequent multi-turn, and a context that keeps growing** (source: §1).

![Characteristics of agentic workloads and the degradation of existing speculative decoding](/assets/images/papers/2610.23787/agent-bb8b8580.webp)

The figure above (Fig. 1 in the original) shows the problem along two axes. Panel (a) shows how agentic workloads are composed, and panel (b) shows how the acceptance length of existing methods collapses on them. The two limitations the authors emphasize are as follows.

1. **Algorithmic degradation.** Methods that achieved high acceptance length on general benchmarks such as GSM8K and MBPP drop to **1–2 accepted tokens** on agentic workloads. That corresponds to a performance loss of more than 80% (source: §1, Fig. 1(b)). It means a lightweight drafter struggles to model history independently well enough to keep up with the target model's distribution.
2. **System inefficiency.** Existing drafters must write accepted tokens back into their own KV cache after verification and retain history for prefix reuse. When the authors measured DeepSeek-V4's MTP module with SGLang, this KV cache update/maintenance cost accounted for **10%–20%** of end-to-end latency (source: §1, §2.2).

The common cause of both problems is the **growing history context**. The authors diagnose the redundancy of "the drafter repeatedly reconstructing information the target model has already processed" as the fundamental inefficiency (source: §2.2).

![Comparison of the speculative decoding pipeline and existing drafter architectures](/assets/images/papers/2610.23787/background2-7e391277.webp)

Fig. 2 in the original lays out this background. (a) is the draft-verify pipeline, (b)(c)(d) are the three existing drafter paradigms, and (e) shows where DLite sits. Existing drafters have evolved from **autoregressive (EAGLE)** to **parallel (DFlash)** to **semi-autoregressive (DSpark, Domino)**, but all three share the property of "explicitly depending on the history context." DLite, as in (e), breaks this dependency by reusing context features produced by the target model (source: §2.1).

## A New Approach: DLite

DLite separates "history modeling" from "speculative generation." Global context modeling is left to the target model, and the drafter performs only local prediction on top of the target's compressed hidden states (source: §3).

![DLite's inference dataflow](/assets/images/papers/2610.23787/overview2-beb8d083.webp)

Fig. 3 in the original is the full dataflow. While the target model processes context and maintains its own KV cache, DLite receives the target hidden states, the current anchor token, and K draft placeholders on every speculative round, passes them through the parallel backbone and then the sequential head to produce candidate tokens, and the target verifies them through the standard procedure. Throughout this process DLite **maintains no draft KV cache of any history** (source: §3.1).

### History-Free Parallel Backbone

The backbone's input comes in two main streams.

**Target hidden states.** DLite extracts hidden states $h_t^{(l)}$ from several layers of the target model and uses them as compressed context features. Because their origin differs from ordinary token embeddings, a learnable depth embedding $e_{\mathrm{depth}}^{(l)}$ distinguishes the source layer (source: §3.1):

$$z_t^{(l)} = h_t^{(l)} + e_{\mathrm{depth}}^{(l)}$$

**Local prediction.** Existing drafters depend on an ever-growing history and global positions, but DLite looks only at the current anchor token and K speculative positions, and uses local position embeddings instead of a global cumulative position. History information is conveyed through the target hidden states, so the drafter does not need to explicitly reconstruct history (source: §3.1).

### Lightweight Sequential Head

Because the parallel backbone produces K positions simultaneously, intra-block autoregressive dependency is lost. At position $T$, the target model conditions on the whole of $x_{\lt t+T}$, but the parallel backbone cannot see the preceding speculative tokens $x_{t:t+T-1}$. DLite points out that "the missing information is exactly this intra-block prefix $x_{t:t+T-1}$ and nothing else," and compresses only this prefix, not the entire history, with a low-rank RNN (source: §3.1):

$$s_T = g_{\phi}\left(s_{T-1}, E(x_{t+T-1})\right)$$

$$q_{\theta,\phi}\left(x_{t+T} \mid h_T, s_T\right) \approx p_T\left(x_{t+T} \mid x_{\lt t}, x_{t:t+T-1}\right)$$

Here $h_T$ encodes the context the target has already processed, and $s_T$ summarizes the missing intra-block prefix. The important design difference is that **sequential modeling happens directly in hidden space**. Existing semi-autoregressive methods first project every backbone representation into $D\times V$ vocabulary logits and then refine with a sequential module, whereas DLite applies the vocabulary projection only once **after** incorporating intra-block dependencies. This avoids repeatedly applying a $D\times V$ projection to large batches of hidden states in high-concurrency serving (source: §3.1).

### Two-Stage Training Strategy

The drafter's goal is to approximate the target distribution, and the more the distributions overlap, the higher the acceptance probability. A distribution-level objective such as KL divergence is therefore a natural choice. Yet the authors show experimentally that **direct KL distillation from the target to the parallel backbone fails** (source: Fig. 4(b)). The cause is the heterogeneity of the prediction structure: the target is an autoregressive predictor over the full history, while the backbone is a parallel predictor over compressed features.

![Sequential head structure and loss/acceptance curves for direct KL/CE distillation](/assets/images/papers/2610.23787/training-fd7dc2b7.webp)

Fig. 4 in the original shows (a) the structure of the sequential head and (b) the problem with direct distillation. In (b), the KL/CE loss fails to descend well, which motivates the two-stage strategy.

**Warm-up stage.** A "history-aware teacher" that is isomorphic to DLite but has a history KV cache is first trained with token-level CE, and then used as an intermediate distillation target to train DLite with forward KL (source: §3.2):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{warmup}} = \sum_{i=1}^{K} w_i\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_i^{H} \,\|\, q_i\right), \quad w_i = \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{i-1}{\gamma}\right)$$

Because the teacher and DLite share the same architecture, their distributions align naturally. The teacher serves as the optimization bridge between the "autoregressive target" and the "history-free drafter."

**Stabilization stage.** Since the final goal is to match the target model, the teacher is discarded and the drafter is optimized directly with a combination of token-level CE and total variation (TV) distance (source: §3.2):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stable}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{CE}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TV}}, \quad \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TV}} = \sum_i w_i \left\| p_i^{T} - q_i \right\|_1$$

## How It Works: A Concrete Example

> The following is an **illustrative example** to aid understanding, not an experimental result from the paper. The block length is reduced to $K=3$ to show the flow.

Suppose we are predicting the token after the sentence "the quick brown fox." Assume the anchor token is the most recently accepted `"fox"`, and that the target model has already processed the entire prompt and produced hidden states $H$.

| Step | Input | Operation | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Target context processing | Full prompt | Target encodes history (drafter not involved) | Hidden states $H$ |
| 2. Parallel backbone | $H$ + depth embedding + `"fox"` + placeholders $M_{1:3}$ | Compute 3 positions simultaneously with bidirectional attention | $h_1, h_2, h_3$ |
| 3. Sequential head $T=1$ | $h_1$, $s_1 = g(s_0, E(\text{fox}))$ | Restore intra-block causality | `"jumps"` |
| 4. Sequential head $T=2$ | $h_2$, $s_2 = g(s_1, E(\text{jumps}))$ | Incorporate only the preceding token | `"over"` |
| 5. Sequential head $T=3$ | $h_3$, $s_3 = g(s_2, E(\text{over}))$ | — | `"the"` |
| 6. Target verification | `"jumps over the"` | Parallel verification in a single forward | Acceptance length $\tau=3$ |

The key is step 2. The backbone produces all 3 positions **at once** (parallel), and the sequential head recursively incorporates **only the immediately preceding token** (lightweight) to restore intra-block causality. The full prompt history is stored nowhere in the drafter. With this, whether the context is 1,000 tokens or 100,000 tokens, the input size the drafter sees is **fixed** at $H$ + anchor + $K$ (source: §3.1).

## Performance: Main Results

Experiments used Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B as targets on NVIDIA H800 GPUs. Baselines were vanilla autoregressive decoding plus EAGLE-3, DFlow, DFlash, DFlash2, and DSpark. All methods used block size $K=7$ (source: §4.1).

### General Workloads

We first compare acceptance length $\tau$ on general benchmarks. DLite consistently matches or beats existing drafters at both model sizes (source: Tab. 1).

| Task group | Benchmark | Metric | DLite 4B | DSpark 4B | DLite 8B | DSpark 8B |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Math | GSM8K | $\tau$ | **6.38** | 6.27 | **6.48** | 6.36 |
| Math | MATH-500 | $\tau$ | **6.30** | 6.25 | **6.35** | 6.34 |
| Code | HumanEval | $\tau$ | **5.68** | 5.62 | **5.96** | 5.86 |
| Code | MBPP | $\tau$ | **5.64** | 5.48 | **5.65** | 5.36 |
| Code | LiveCodeBench | $\tau$ | **5.31** | 5.20 | **5.66** | 5.43 |
| Chat | MT-Bench | $\tau$ | **3.94** | 3.81 | **3.96** | 3.88 |
| Chat | Alpaca | $\tau$ | **3.64** | 3.54 | **3.78** | 3.64 |

The improvement on general benchmarks is **modest**. That is because this is the regime where existing drafters already achieve high acceptance length. The authors anticipate that "the gap widens as context modeling becomes harder," and confirm this in the long-context and agentic experiments (source: §4.2).

### Long-Context Workloads

On the four categories of LongBench v2, DLite's advantage becomes clear. With Qwen3-4B, Code Repo Ud reaches a **2.66×** speedup versus DSpark's $1.81\times$, and acceptance length rises from 2.44 to 3.14 (source: Tab. 2). For Qwen3-8B, the speedups across the four categories are **2.32×** on Multi-Doc QA, **2.74×** on Long In-ctx, **2.53×** on Code Repo, and **3.96×** on Long-dialog (source: Tab. 2). The authors' interpretation is that as context grows, existing drafters must rely on modeling their own history, whereas DLite delegates this to the target and performs only local prediction (source: §4.2).

### Agentic Workloads

The relative gains are largest on agentic benchmarks. Acceptance length in particular jumps substantially over DSpark on SWE-Bench (source: Tab. 3).

| Benchmark | DLite $\tau$ (4B/8B) | DSpark $\tau$ (4B/8B) | DLite speedup (4B/8B) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| AgentMemBench | **2.80** / **2.69** | 2.10 / 2.26 | **2.39×** / **2.20×** |
| AgentLongBench | **2.51** / **2.79** | 1.84 / 2.09 | **2.07×** / **2.31×** |
| BFCL-v3 | **2.78** / **2.76** | 2.54 / 2.23 | **2.37×** / **2.30×** |
| SWE-Bench | **3.15** / **3.19** | 1.89 / 1.94 | **2.68×** / **2.74×** |

On SWE-Bench, Qwen3-4B's acceptance length improves from DSpark's 1.89 to DLite's 3.15, about a 66.7% improvement, which is the basis for the paper's claim of "up to 70% over DSpark" (source: Tab. 3, §5). This shows that the history-free design is particularly advantageous in agent execution, which is characterized by long, evolving contexts.

### High-Concurrency Throughput

Throughput was evaluated at concurrency 1–64. DLite recorded higher throughput than DSpark at both model sizes and across all workloads (source: Tab. 4). Notably, **throughput gains persist even when acceptance lengths are similar**. For example, on a single Qwen3-4B LCB request, DLite reaches 960 TPS (3.97×) versus DSpark's 905 TPS (3.74×), which comes from the lightweight sequential head avoiding an intermediate $D\times V$ projection and thus keeping draft overhead low (source: Tab. 4, §3.1).

### Ablation

![Ablation on training strategy and KV cache](/assets/images/papers/2610.23787/ablation-0d71c141.webp)

Fig. 5 in the original shows two ablations.

- **(a) Training strategy.** Two-stage training maintains higher acceptance length throughout the stabilization stage than single-stage direct training and also improves final performance. This supports the claim that the history-aware teacher is an "optimization bridge" (source: Fig. 5(a), §4.3).
- **(b) History KV cache.** After training sliding-window KV cache variants of 128 and 512 tokens on the same data and settings, acceptance length converges across the three models once training is sufficient. In other words, history KV helps early in training, but the benefit disappears once the drafter learns to use the context information conveyed through the target hidden states (source: Fig. 5(b), §4.3).

The design-space exploration (appendix) covers three choices (source: Appx. B): increasing the number of target hidden states from 7 to 14 raises average $\tau$ from 3.14 to 3.30, but saturates at 20 and 36 (3.29/3.27); sequence-level combination (3.30) is better than feature-level combination (3.04); and increasing the sequential head rank from 256 to 512 raises performance from 3.16 to 3.30, but even 4096 (full $D$) only reaches 3.33. Based on these observations the authors chose the final configuration (14 target hidden states, sequence-level combination, rank 512) (source: Appx. B).

## Our Take: Strengths, Limitations, and Why This Work Matters

**Strengths.** The paper's biggest contribution is that it squarely removes the long-standing implicit assumption that "the drafter reconstructs history." It is elegant in that it binds two seemingly different problems—acceptance length (algorithm quality) and KV cache overhead (system efficiency)—into **a single root cause**, the growing history, and solves both with one design principle (source: §2.2, §5). The empirical results support this claim well: the history-free design does not lose ground on general workloads (it is in fact slightly ahead) while clearly leading on long-context and agentic workloads (source: Tab. 1–3). The fact that the choice to "do sequential modeling in hidden space" translates into throughput gains under high concurrency is also significant from a practical serving standpoint.

**Limitations — acknowledged by the authors.** The authors state explicitly that "training a history-free drafter requires a two-stage strategy." This is the price of the design's simplicity: the structures are heterogeneous enough that direct distillation fails, so an additional training pipeline is needed—teacher training (8 epochs) + KL distillation (2 epochs) + stabilization training (6 epochs) (source: §3.2, Appx. A). The authors themselves also acknowledge that the improvement on general benchmarks is "marginal" (source: §4.2).

**Limitations — reviewer's inference.** Several unverified conditions remain. First, because DLite depends heavily on target hidden states, in heterogeneous serving scenarios where the target and drafter are distributed across **different hardware/devices**, a new cost of transmitting hidden states may appear. The paper only covers the dataflow on a single H800. Second, generalization was not tested for cases where the target architecture changes to something like MoE, or where the number of hidden-state extraction layers varies with target depth. Third, cost metrics such as the drafter's total parameter count and training FLOPs are not reported in the original, making it hard to independently reproduce and verify "how much cheaper removing history actually is."

**Applicability.** For **agent serving** characterized by long input, short output, multi-turn interaction, and accumulating context, DLite is a strong candidate. Conversely, on simple workloads centered on single-turn conversation and short prompts, the gains are not large, and there is no big difference from well-tuned EAGLE-style methods (source: Tab. 1). The value of the history-free design shows when "the history is long and keeps growing."

## Next Steps: The Road Ahead

The authors do not explicitly propose future directions. But in light of the limitations, reasonable next steps are as follows.

- **Re-evaluation under heterogeneous deployment.** Research is needed to quantify whether hidden-state transmission bandwidth becomes a bottleneck when the target and drafter are deployed separately.
- **Extension to more diverse targets.** The generality of the number of extracted hidden states and the combination scheme should be checked on MoE targets and deeper models (e.g., 70B-class).
- **Cost transparency.** Reporting drafter parameter counts, training FLOPs, and energy/cost metrics would allow independent verification of the system-level benefits of "eliminating the KV cache."
- **Learning without a teacher.** To reduce the complexity of two-stage training, there is room to explore objective functions or curricula that stabilize direct distillation between heterogeneous structures.
